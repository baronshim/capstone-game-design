|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | 0:20 | Hi, I'm Baron. This is For the Record, a chat game where six voices go on the record, one is lying, and some are machines. The poster QR is live; I'll leave it up at the end. |
| 2 | The book | 0:45 | Where the idea comes from. Alex records his life for aliens who will never meet him and copies the Voyager Golden Record, a portrait of Earth with the war and cruelty cut out. The book keeps showing him choosing what goes in; the hospital is exactly what the aliens don't get. My theme: the curated self versus the actual self. |
| 3 | Why this idea | 0:45 | I started from the party game Imposter (one player doesn't know the word and bluffs through clues) and added a Turing test: some seats are AI bots mimicking the humans in the room. Both are the theme as a mechanic: the imposter performs knowledge it lacks, the bots edit themselves to blend in, and everyone curates a self for people who can't check. The feeling I wanted is the small betrayal at the reveal. |
| 4 | One round | 0:45 | Create a room, share a four-letter code; empty seats fill with bots. Six seconds to read your card. Two passes of one-word clues. 150 seconds of open chat where the bots argue too. A vote; an ejected imposter gets one guess at the word. Then everyone marks every seat human or bot. Then the reveal. |
| 5 | The reveal | 0:30 | The reveal is the uncurated record: who was human, who was a machine, who was lying, who voted for whom. Everyone scores, bots included, so someone always wins, and it's often not who you thought. |
| 6 | Process | 0:50 | Plan, setup, build. I wrote the spec with Claude in a question-and-answer session, then each milestone got a written plan with one task per piece. Each task: failing test, make it pass, commit, review. A stronger model reviewed each whole milestone; then I deployed and played it live, and what we learned went back into the spec. 82 commits, 213 tests that run inside the real Cloudflare runtime, three milestones and one dropped on purpose. |
| 7 | Prompts | 0:50 | Real prompts, typos included. Mine are short because the AI asked the questions and I made the calls. "Lets do C with an unknown amount of bots" is how the game got six seats where nobody knows how many are human. "Forget the whole knights and knaves" is the biggest pivot. "Bots should have their own thinking" is me rejecting a design the AI proposed. "Way too much text" is the poster. |
| 8 | Interim versions | 0:40 | Day 5 a chat room with animal names, the MVP. Day 11 the imposter round and, the same night, bots on Workers AI, live on the web. Then two playtest passes, the rename and poster, the tutorial and feedback button. Classmates could play from day 11, which gave me two weeks of real feedback. |
| 9 | Where it worked | 0:50 | Playtest one gave me six bullet points. I pasted them in; the AI traced each to a cause in the code and proposed the fix; I said go. A day later: a deal phase, longer timers, banners, a progress bar, typing indicators for every seat (so the indicator isn't itself a tell), sound, and bots that wait, type at human speed, and talk more. Also: the end-of-milestone review found four real bugs the task reviews missed, including an error message that leaked the secret word. |
| 10 | Where it was less helpful | 0:55 | The first live deploy had silent bots with every test green: the model defaults to a thinking mode that spent the whole token budget reasoning and returned an empty answer, so every bot quietly fell back to a script. Only logging the raw reply in production found it. Second, a red herring: local timers fired late, the "fix" made it worse, production was fine all along. Third, a design I rejected: to stop bots voting as a bloc, the AI proposed dealing each bot a random suspect. That's a fake opinion. I made them form their own read from the clues and stay open to being convinced. |
| 11 | Game jam feedback | 0:40 | In the players' words: names blend, so add icons. Some screens scroll. Bots open with your name too often and only send one line. Anyone can press start. And the chat is too long now: playtest one said too fast, I went 90 to 150 seconds, game day says too long. The answer is in between. |
| 12 | Next two weeks | 0:40 | Icons and sharper names first, because the vote and the bot call depend on remembering who said what. Chat to about two minutes, no name-first lines, short two-line bursts. Then a responsive pass and host-only start. The stretch I wanted from the beginning: an annotated replay at the reveal, the transcript marked with which human each bot was copying. The record, and what was cut. |

Likely questions:

- *Is the AI ever the imposter?* Yes, any of the six seats, uniformly. A bot imposter that is ejected gets a steal guess like a human.
- *How do you stop players from prompt-injecting the bots?* Structurally: bots have no tools and only two secrets (their role, the word); every reply is JSON validated against the game state; any line containing the word is dropped; player chat goes to the model in a delimited data block.
- *What does it cost to run?* Nothing. Workers free plan, Workers AI free daily allocation (about 7 to 9 full rounds a day); past that, bots play by script and the lobby says so.
- *What did you write yourself?* The idea, the spec decisions, every pivot, the playtests, the art direction, the deploys. Claude wrote the code task by task under written plans; the full prompt log is in the write-up.
